## PROGRAM VISION & MISSION

### Vision

Nahua youth from highland communities in Veracruz and Hidalgo are educated, employed, and proud of their identity — using sport as the discipline, structure, and institutional gateway that makes this possible.

### Mission

To develop scholar-athletes from Nahua/Macehuali communities by combining world-class athletic training with rigorous academic integration, so that sport becomes the pathway to university education, career opportunity, and community leadership — not merely the destination.

### Core Belief

Elite athletic achievement is the exceptional outcome for the few. Disciplined, educated, employed adults who carry their cultural identity with pride is the standard outcome for all. Sport is the vehicle; the destination is a life well-lived.

### What This Program Is Not

This is not a talent factory. It is not a program that identifies the most athletically gifted youth and abandons the rest. It is not an externally managed project. It is a Nahua community institution that uses athletic excellence as proof of what structured effort produces.

## TARGET POPULATION

### Communities

Primary target: Nahua-speaking communities in highland zones of:
- Veracruz: Sierra de Zongolica (1,200–2,700m), Sierra Norte de Veracruz, Totonacapan highlands
- Hidalgo: Sierra Alta Hidalguense (1,400–2,100m), Huasteca Hidalguense highland communities

Secondary target: Nahua communities in adjacent lowland zones as the program matures.

### Demographics

Youth ages 12–22, with primary focus on the 12–18 window (highest developmental leverage per the literature). Boys first cohort; girls second cohort once community trust is established and gender-appropriate spaces created.

### Physiological Profile (Research-Based)

Adult Nahua men from these communities average 158–164 cm and 60–70 kg. Key characteristics:
- Longer torso / shorter legs (Brachyskelic) — lower center of gravity
- Well-developed postural and grip musculature from mecapal load-carrying and milpa farming
- Highland altitude adaptation (1,200–2,700m): naturally higher hemoglobin, red blood cell mass, and VO2 max than sea-level populations — equivalent to elite altitude training camps
- Mixed Type I / Type IIa muscle fiber profile — suits power-endurance sports and middle-distance running
- Lean muscle-to-bodyweight ratio high relative to stature

This profile is a natural match for: wrestling, boxing, judo, weightlifting, middle-distance and long-distance running, road cycling.

### Socioeconomic Context

- Household income: low; many families in subsistence agriculture or informal labor
- Male out-migration to cities: high — many households headed by women
- School infrastructure: telebachillerato and telesecundaria systems present; completion rates below national average
- Indigenous language: Nahuatl (varying dialects by zone); Spanish bilingualism variable
- Stunting rate: 20–35% in this region's indigenous communities (ENSANUT data) — critical for program design
- Iron deficiency anemia: 20–35% of women and girls — must be screened before fitness testing

### Cultural Assets

- Nahua running tradition: tameme messenger-runners covered 400+ km/day in relay; running is culturally honored
- Tlachtli/ulama ballgame tradition: explosive hip rotation, lateral agility, sustained aerobic effort — culturally embedded athletic heritage
- Xochiyaoyotl (ritualized combat): formalized combat with rules, honors, and social recognition — cultural legitimacy for combat sports
- Mecapal load-carrying: 30–60 kg over mountain terrain — natural athletic conditioning base
- Tequio (communal labor): community investment ethic directly applicable to facility building
- Sistema de cargos: communal governance of responsibilities — institutional infrastructure for program sustainability

## THEORY OF CHANGE

### The Evidence Base

This program is built on the Sport-for-Development (S4D) academic literature, with specific reference to:
- Coalter, F. (2007) — *A Wider Social Role for Sport* (Routledge): the foundational "sport-plus" framework
- Gould & Carson (2008) — life skills transfer requires explicit instruction, not assumed emergence
- Clontarf Foundation (Australia) — indigenous sport-for-development; most rigorously evaluated comparable program
- Holt, N.L. (ed., 2016) — *Positive Youth Development Through Sport* (Routledge)

### Sport-Plus, Not Plus-Sport

The central design principle from the evidence base: life skills do NOT emerge automatically from sport participation. A disciplined wrestler is not automatically a disciplined student. The program must explicitly teach, name, and reinforce the connection between athletic development and life outcomes.

Every training session includes a brief teachable moment. Every month each athlete has a one-on-one conversation with their coach connecting sport progress to academic and career goals.

### The Mechanism Chain

Sport participation
→ Structured daily routine + mandatory school attendance tie-in
→ Coach-athlete relationship (primary adult mentor for many youth)
→ Explicit life-skill instruction (goal-setting, resilience, emotional regulation)
→ Academic engagement and completion
→ University access via scholarships or state programs
→ Career entry and economic mobility
→ Community leadership and program alumni mentorship

### The Clontarf Inversion

The Clontarf Foundation (Australia) — the best-evidenced indigenous sport-for-development program — demonstrates the key design principle: "No school → no training." Not "no passing grades → no participation." The bar is school attendance, not academic performance. Requiring grades disqualifies the most vulnerable; requiring attendance creates the accountability loop that improves grades over time.

### Cultural Identity as a Development Mechanism

Dudgeon et al. (2010) — the strongest single protective factor for indigenous youth against dropout, substance use, and depression is cultural identity strength — stronger than economic support, program quality, or family structure. Nahuatl language use in coaching, elder involvement, and connection to traditional Nahua physical culture are not decorative. They are active development ingredients.

### The Transfer Bridge

Life skills transfer from sport to academics requires explicit bridging by coaches. Without it, sport produces athletes but not scholars. With it, the same training session produces both. The bridge is simple: "The goal-setting process you used to improve your takedown percentage — we're going to use the exact same process for your math grade."

## SPORT SELECTION & RATIONALE

### Primary Sports

#### Wrestling (Freestyle & Greco-Roman)
The highest-priority sport for this program, for five converging reasons:
1. Physical profile match: compact body, lower COG, high relative grip strength, hip power — ideal wrestling attributes
2. Weight classes eliminate height disadvantage entirely
3. Cultural resonance: Nahua Xochiyaoyotl (ritualized combat with rules/honors) is a direct cultural antecedent
4. NCAA scholarship gap: 125–133 lb weight classes (57–60 kg) are chronically undersupplied — the exact weight range of Nahua males
5. CONADE infrastructure: Mexico has Federación Mexicana de Lucha and state-level programs in Veracruz and Hidalgo

#### Distance Running (Middle & Long Distance)
The second-priority sport, especially for highland communities above 1,500m:
1. Zero infrastructure cost — dirt roads and mountain terrain are the training facility
2. Altitude adaptation is a documented, recruitable physiological advantage — US coaches actively seek it
3. Cultural resonance: tameme running tradition; messenger-runners as honored occupational role in Nahua culture
4. Prize money at Mexican road races: elite runners earn MXN $100,000–$500,000/year from road racing alone
5. Rarámuri (Tarahumara) parallel: same altitude, same stature, same maize diet — documented elite running potential

#### Boxing (48–60 kg classes)
The third primary sport:
1. Physical profile match: compact, explosive rotational power from hip
2. Mexico has the deepest boxing culture in Latin America — coach availability is the best of any sport in rural areas
3. At-risk male 12–16 engagement: combat sports provide a sanctioned channel for physical status-seeking (documented in literature)
4. Professional pathway: viable career income from regional boxing at ages 20–26
5. Cultural resonance: formalized combat with honor codes aligns with Nahua warrior traditions

### Secondary Sports (Tier 2)

#### Judo
- Identical physical profile benefits to wrestling
- Lower equipment cost (bare feet, one gi)
- No NCAA scholarships — athletes must convert to wrestling for college pathway or pursue academic scholarships
- IJF development programs available through Federación Mexicana de Judo
- Japanese university scholarship pathway (MEXT) for Pan-American level judoka

#### Road Cycling (Highland Communities)
- Altitude adaptation translates directly to road cycling performance
- Colombia/Ecuador Andes model: Carapaz, Quintana, Chaves — all from poor rural highland communities
- Hidalgo highlands (Mineral del Chico, Huasca de Ocampo) and Zongolica provide ideal training terrain
- Equipment cost is the main barrier; partnership with FMC (Federación Mexicana de Ciclismo) for equipment access

#### Weightlifting
- Compact physique with high strength-to-weight ratio: textbook weightlifting body
- Mexico's historically strongest Olympic sport (Soraya Jiménez, 2000 gold)
- Can be set up in a garage with ~$1,500 of equipment
- No NCAA scholarships — academic pathway only

### Multi-Sport Approach
Research shows multi-sport programs produce better development outcomes than single-sport programs. The primary sports (wrestling, running, boxing) share physical preparation requirements (conditioning, strength, aerobic base), allowing athletes to train multiple sports without overspecialization. Specialization happens at age 15–16, not at age 12.

### Sports Explicitly Not Prioritized
- Basketball, volleyball: height disadvantage is structural
- Swimming: pool infrastructure required — not accessible in highland communities
- Soccer: highly competitive at national level; limited scholarship opportunities relative to effort required
- Judo, boxing, weightlifting as primary sports for college pathway: none have NCAA scholarships (wrestling is the only combat sport with NCAA scholarships)

## THE SCHOLAR-ATHLETE MODEL

### Core Requirement: School Enrollment

Program participation is conditional on school enrollment. Not passing grades — enrollment and attendance. An athlete who drops out of school cannot train with the program. This creates the bidirectional accountability loop documented in the Clontarf Foundation evaluation.

### Academic Integration Architecture

Study hall before practice (mandatory, 60 minutes):
- Location: at the training facility, supervised by academic mentor
- Content: homework completion, test preparation, reading
- Coach monitors completion before athletes access the gym/track
- This is the single most-replicated effective design feature across multiple documented programs

Monthly coach-academic review:
- Coach receives school attendance and grade report from teacher partner
- Monthly 15-minute individual conversation with each athlete
- Explicit bridge-building: sport goals ↔ academic goals using the same frameworks

Teacher-coach communication protocol:
- Formal partnership agreement with local telebachillerato / telesecundaria
- Teacher notifies coach of attendance or performance issues within 48 hours
- Coach follows up — creating the "two adults watching" effect documented to significantly improve student effort

### Scholarship Pathway Communication

From enrollment day, athletes understand the full pathway available to them:
- IDERV/CODEH state sport beca: MXN $1,500–$4,000/month (achievable at state competition level)
- CONADE SINADE beca Nivel I–II: MXN $2,000–$9,000/month (national competition level)
- Universidad Veracruzana or UAEH athletic scholarship: MXN $1,500–$3,500/month
- Beca Bienestar Benito Juárez: MXN $2,400/month (university level, stackable)
- Combined university package: MXN $5,400–$9,400/month — sufficient to live and study in Xalapa or Pachuca
- SEDENA military athlete: MXN $9,500–$12,000/month + full medical (best option without international results)
- NCAA D2/NAIA wrestling scholarship: full ride possible for athletes who reach Pan-American Youth level
- IOC Olympic Solidarity scholarship: USD $1,500–$2,500/month for LA 2028 cycle

Alumni who have used these pathways visit the program regularly and speak concretely about their experience. Abstract aspiration does not motivate; lived proof does.

### Life Skills Curriculum

Embedded in coaching, not delivered as a separate class. Based on Danish's Going for the Goal (GOAL) framework — the most rigorously tested life skills through sport curriculum, with multiple RCTs showing improvements in goal-setting behavior, academic planning, and reduced drug use.

Core skills explicitly taught and named:
1. Goal-setting: SMART goals for sport, then bridged to SMART goals for school
2. Resilience: using setbacks in training as explicit resilience practice; journaling (optional) or verbal reflection
3. Emotional regulation: pre-competition routines; managing anger in contact sports
4. Communication: asking for help from coach, teacher, community elder
5. Planning: weekly training plan → weekly study plan; same cognitive process

Teachable moment model (Danish, 1997): use sport events (a loss, a breakthrough, an injury recovery) as leverage points for explicit life-skill instruction. Coach names the lesson in real time.

## PROGRAM DESIGN — AGE STAGES

### Stage 1: Foundation (Ages 12–15) — "Tlapaloa" (Beginning)

Goals: Physical literacy, sport enjoyment, school attendance, cultural identity, basic life skills introduction

Weekly structure:
- 5 days/week training (after school, 2.5 hours: 60 min study hall + 90 min sport)
- 1 day/week community session (elder involvement, Nahuatl language, cultural content)
- School attendance monitored weekly

Sport content: Multi-sport introduction — all three primary sports sampled. No specialization. Fun, mastery-oriented, effort rewarded not results. Team/pair activities emphasized for social bonding.

Academic requirements: School enrollment and attendance only. No grade floor — support struggling students rather than exclude them.

Life skills focus: Goal-setting, effort vs. outcome, team belonging.

Cultural content: Nahuatl names for all movements and concepts used alongside Spanish. Stories of Nahua athletic traditions embedded in coaching. Visit from community elders monthly.

Key milestone: By age 15, athlete has competed in at least one municipal/state sport event and has not dropped out of school.

---

### Stage 2: Development (Ages 15–18) — "Icuilioa" (Writing / Forming)

Goals: Sport specialization, academic continuation, state/national competition entry, scholarship pathway activation

Weekly structure:
- 6 days/week training (5 weekdays + Saturday competition/technique session)
- Study hall mandatory before every weekday session
- Monthly coach-academic review with school records
- Quarterly community showcase (families, elders, community members see athlete progress)

Sport content: Athletes choose primary sport at age 15 (with coach guidance). Secondary sport maintained for cross-training. Compete in Juegos Deportivos Estatales (state games), Universiada Nacional pathway begins if enrolled in university/prep. Registration with national federation.

Academic requirements: School enrollment; academic mentor assigned; teacher-coach communication protocol active.

Life skills focus: Resilience, leadership, long-term planning, identity beyond athlete.

Scholarship activation: State sport beca application through IDERV or CODEH. CONADE Juegos Nacionales target. World Athletics registration for runners (free, permanent, searchable internationally).

Cultural content: Nahuatl used in advanced coaching conversations (not just terms). Athletes participate in community ceremonies as athletes — public identity as Nahua scholar-athletes, not just sport participants.

Key milestone: By age 18, athlete has competed at state level and has maintained continuous school enrollment.

---

### Stage 3: High Performance / Transition (Ages 18–22) — "Tlahtoa" (Speaking Out)

Goals: University enrollment or professional transition, national/international competition, community leadership development, alumni integration

Track A — University: UV or UAEH enrollment + stacked scholarship package (Bienestar + athletic + state beca = MXN $5,400–$9,400/month). National competition. COM/Olympic Solidarity nomination pipeline for LA 2028 cycle.

Track B — Military: SEDENA Soldado Deportista enlistment (MXN $9,500–$12,000/month + full medical). CISM international competition. Parallel completion of secondary education if not yet complete.

Track C — Professional sport: Boxing professional license at 20+ (after building amateur record). Combate Global/MMA pathway for wrestlers/boxers. Road racing prize money for distance runners.

Alumni integration: Stage 3 athletes become near-peer mentors for Stage 1 and 2 athletes. This is a formal program role, not informal. They receive a small stipend or in-kind support for this service, creating the first step of a career in sport leadership.

Community leadership: Athletes in Stage 3 begin taking on community responsibilities — organizing junior training sessions, liaising with school administrators, representing the program in community governance. This is the cargo model applied to sport.

## COACHING & MENTORSHIP ARCHITECTURE

### Primary Coaches: Community Members First

The evidence is clear (Anderson, 2009; Clontarf Foundation evaluations): coaches who share cultural background and language with athletes produce significantly better outcomes than technically superior external coaches.

Hiring criteria for primary coaches:
1. From the Nahua community (ideally same municipality or adjacent)
2. Nahuatl speaker
3. Genuine care for youth — not credentials
4. Basic physical literacy; willingness to learn
5. Community trust and relationships

Technical expertise is developed through training, not hired in. The community coach is the primary relationship. The external technical expert is a training resource.

### Technical Coaches: Training the Trainers

External technical coaches (state federation coaches, CONADE-certified coaches) serve the community coaches, not the athletes directly. Their role:
- Monthly technical training sessions with community coaches
- Annual sport clinics for athletes (3–5 days)
- Technique review via video (WhatsApp video is sufficient at community level)
- Assessment of athletes for state-level competition readiness

This structure avoids the dependency and collapse pattern (programs built around an external expert who leaves).

### Coach Training Requirements (Community Coaches)

Minimum training before taking a youth cohort:
1. Child protection / safeguarding basics (3 hours) — CONADE has this curriculum
2. Positive coaching principles (3 hours) — autonomy-supportive, mastery-oriented coaching
3. Life skills facilitation (GOAL framework, 6 hours)
4. Sport-specific technique module (varies by sport, 16–40 hours)
5. Academic integration protocol (1 hour) — the study hall, teacher communication, bridge-building

Total: ~30–50 hours. Deliverable over 2 weekends. Not a credential program — a functional preparation.

### Mentorship Network Architecture

Each athlete has a minimum 3-adult network:
1. Community coach (daily relationship, Nahuatl-speaking, primary attachment figure)
2. Academic mentor (teacher partner or volunteer tutor — quarterly contact minimum)
3. Near-peer alumni mentor (program graduate 3–5 years older, same background)

Athletes who lack adult male figures at home (high incidence in communities with out-migration) have the community coach as their primary male role model. This is documented as one of the highest-value functions of the program for this demographic. The coach must understand and accept this responsibility.

### Female Athlete Considerations

When the program expands to girls:
- Female community coaches are required for female cohorts
- Gender-segregated training is the default in rural Nahua communities
- Father/male guardian endorsement must be obtained for female participants
- Female elders involved in program governance alongside male elders
- Traditional female physical roles in Nahua culture (cihuatl ceremonial runners, etc.) should be invoked to legitimize participation with conservative families

## COMMUNITY GOVERNANCE — EJIDO MODEL

### Why the Ejido System Is the Sustainability Architecture

Bolivia's Andean programs using ayllu community governance (the structural equivalent of the ejido/cargo system) showed dramatically better 10-year sustainability than externally managed programs. The ejido model is not just a legal mechanism — it is the community's own governance infrastructure applied to a new function.

### Governance Structure

Ejido Assembly:
- Approves use of communal land for program facilities
- Reviews program outcomes annually
- Votes on major decisions (coach hiring, facility expansion, external partnerships)

Sport Cargo Position:
- A recognized community responsibility within the sistema de cargos
- Person holding the sport cargo manages day-to-day program operations
- The cargo rotates per community custom, but program institutional memory is maintained through documentation and alumni networks
- This creates accountability without external supervision

Tequio for Construction:
- Facility construction and maintenance through communal labor (tequio)
- This creates collective investment and ownership that no externally built facility achieves
- Community members who contribute tequio have a stake in the facility's success

### Asset Ownership

All physical assets (land, training space, equipment) are owned by the ejido, not by any individual, external organization, or government program. External funding buys equipment and coach training — not the facility or the land. This is the structural guarantee against program collapse when external funding ends.

### Program Council

Five-person program council accountable to the ejido assembly:
1. Sport cargo holder (community governance representative)
2. Head community coach (technical program responsibility)
3. School liaison (telebachillerato teacher or principal)
4. Elder representative (cultural integrity accountability)
5. Alumni representative (near-peer voice, program continuity)

Decisions require 3 of 5 votes. The ejido assembly can override any program council decision.

## NUTRITION & HEALTH INTEGRATION

### The Critical Pre-Condition: Iron Screening

34–44% stunting rate and 20–35% iron deficiency anemia in this population (ENSANUT data). An anemic athlete will test falsely low on every aerobic fitness measure. Iron deficiency must be screened and addressed before any fitness testing begins.

Iron deficiency protocol:
1. Hemoglobin screening at enrollment (HemoCue finger-prick device, ~$150 one-time cost for the device)
2. Athletes with Hgb below 11 g/dL referred to DIF/IMSS for treatment before intensive training
3. Deworming (albendazole) before iron supplementation — parasitic load causes chronic blood loss, especially in lowland Veracruz
4. Nutrition education: pairing iron-rich foods (quelites) with vitamin C (lime, chili) dramatically increases absorption. This is already embedded in traditional Nahua cuisine — cultural reinforcement, not new behavior.

### The Traditional Diet Is the Right Athletic Diet

The milpa diet (maize + beans + quelites + chilis) is, counterintuitively, an excellent athletic diet: high complex carbohydrates for glycogen loading, complete protein from maize-bean combination, exceptional micronutrient density from quelites (quintonil, verdolaga, huauzontle). The problem is diet erosion — replacement by ultra-processed food (Bimbo bread, Coca-Cola, Maruchan).

Nutrition program:
- School garden milpa program (partner with SEP's indigenous education system)
- Quelites preparation incorporated into program culture (cooking at facility or home with family)
- Explicit messaging: traditional food is athletic food. Modern processed food is not.
- No protein supplement supplements pushed on communities where animal protein is scarce — optimize the plant-protein base first.

### Bone Age Screening for Talent Identification

Some athletes are short due to genetics (appropriate for weight-class sports as-is); others are short due to early childhood stunting (potential for some catch-up growth if nutrition is addressed). Wrist X-ray bone age assessment for athletes identified as having high potential helps distinguish these cases and guides long-term development planning.

## MEASUREMENT & EVALUATION

### Guiding Principle

The academic literature is clear: programs that measure only participation and athletic performance have no credible evidence base. This program tracks education and life outcomes from day one.

### University Research Partnership

In Year 1, establish a formal research partnership with one of:
- CIESAS Golfo (Xalapa, Veracruz) — Centro de Investigaciones y Estudios Superiores en Antropología Social
- Universidad Veracruzana (Xalapa) — School of Physical Culture
- UAEH (Pachuca, Hidalgo) — academic partner in the same altitude zone
- UNAM — national platform for broader publication

The partnership provides:
- Longitudinal database maintenance (participant records, followed over 10+ years)
- Periodic evaluation design and analysis
- Academic publication of outcomes (produces the first peer-reviewed evidence on sport-for-development with highland Nahua youth — a documented research gap)
- Credibility with government funders and international development organizations

### Measurement Layers

Layer 1 — Track Weekly (Low Cost):
- Program attendance by athlete
- School attendance (obtain monthly from school)
- Sport event participation and results

Layer 2 — Track Quarterly (Medium Effort):
- School grade progression (on-grade vs. behind)
- Academic grades by subject
- Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (10-item, 15 minutes, validated internationally)
- Behavioral incidents (coach report)
- Hemoglobin level (annual after initial screening)

Layer 3 — Track Annually (High Value):
- School enrollment continuity
- School level completion (secondary, baccalaureate)
- Tertiary enrollment
- Employment status at age 20, 22, 25
- Scholarship/beca access achieved (which level, when)
- Near-peer mentor role uptake (alumni who return)

### Key Research Questions This Program Can Answer

The literature has documented that no peer-reviewed study exists on sport-for-development with highland Nahua youth. This program, with a university partner and longitudinal tracking, can answer:
1. Does combat sport + running combination produce stronger development outcomes than single-sport programs for this population?
2. Does Nahuatl-language coaching produce measurably better outcomes than Spanish-only coaching?
3. What is the baseline VO2 max, hemoglobin, and anthropometric profile of highland Nahua youth aged 12–18? (No published data exists)
4. Does altitude-adapted physiology produce measurably superior endurance competition outcomes when athletes enter the national system?

## PHASE PLAN

### Phase 1 — Foundation (Months 1–12)
Phase 1: Community Consultation and Governance Setup

Step 1: Community consultation — present program to ejido assembly; obtain formal endorsement; identify sport cargo holder and council members.

Step 2: Site assessment — identify ejido land suitable for training facility; assess telebachillerato partnership viability; confirm state institute (IDERV/CODEH) partnership.

Step 3: Coach recruitment — identify 2–3 community members meeting coach criteria (community trust, care for youth, Nahuatl speaker); do not require sport credentials.

Step 4: Funding activation — file CONADE EIDM application; contact IDERV/CODEH for co-funding; activate INPI facility support; ensure all enrolled youth have Beca Bienestar.

Step 5: Facility construction — tequio-based construction of covered training space; install wrestling mat; set up running routes with distance markers.

Step 6: Coach training — 2-weekend training program: child protection, positive coaching, life skills facilitation, sport technique module.

Step 7: Enrollment — first cohort of 20–30 athletes ages 12–15; health screening (hemoglobin, basic anthropometry); school enrollment verification; family meetings.

Step 8: Program launch — Month 6; first training sessions; Nahuatl naming ceremony for program launch.

---

### Phase 2 — Development (Months 12–36)
Phase 2: First Competition Cycle and Scholarship Activation

Step 1: Municipal/state competition entry — register athletes with sport federations; enter Juegos Deportivos Estatales; document results for state beca applications.

Step 2: State institute partnerships — formalize IDERV/CODEH partnership; initiate state sport beca applications for qualifying athletes.

Step 3: Academic outcome review — 18-month check: school enrollment rates, grade progression, attendance comparison vs. non-program youth in community.

Step 4: Second cohort enrollment — recruit ages 12–15 from adjacent community; first-cohort athletes become near-peer mentors for new enrollees.

Step 5: University partnership establishment — contact CIESAS Golfo or Universidad Veracruzana for research partnership; establish longitudinal tracking database.

Step 6: CONADEPI registration — register program with CONADE's indigenous sport commission; enter Juegos Deportivos Nacionales Indígenas.

Step 7: World Athletics registration — register all distance runners with World Athletics (free); participate in first FMAA-sanctioned road races.

---

### Phase 3 — Maturation (Months 36–60)
Phase 3: National System Integration and Alumni Pipeline

Step 1: National federation integration — top athletes in each sport registered with Federación Mexicana de Lucha / FMAA / FMBE; SINADE Nivel I applications submitted.

Step 2: First university enrollments — Stage 2 athletes (now 18) guided into UV or UAEH enrollment; stacked scholarship package activated.

Step 3: SEDENA pathway activation — age-eligible athletes (18+) who prefer military route guided to 11a/20a Zona Militar contacts.

Step 4: Alumni formal structure — establish alumni association with near-peer mentor roles; first alumni return as paid (stipend) community coaches.

Step 5: Research publication — university partner submits first academic paper on program outcomes; produces evidence base for scaling and further funding.

Step 6: Replication assessment — evaluate readiness to establish second community cohort; document institutional model for replication.

Step 7: COM pipeline — identify top 1–3 athletes with LA 2028 Olympic Solidarity scholarship potential; present to national federation for COM nomination.

---

### Phase 4 — Scale (Year 5+)
Phase 4: Multi-Community Network

Expansion to 3–5 communities in the same highland zone. Each new community follows the same Phase 1–3 sequence. A small regional coordination structure (managed by Phase 1 community alumni) provides:
- Coach training for new communities
- Shared competition transport
- Longitudinal data collection
- Liaison with state institutes and national federations

The program becomes self-replicating through the alumni pipeline. External funding requirements decrease as ejido governance and state institute integration mature. The research partnership produces ongoing evidence that attracts further institutional support.
